\title{OceanSaksham: An Open-Source Architecture for Credibility-Aware Triage and Audit Logging in Crowdsourced Coastal Hazard Management}

\begin{abstract}
Crowdsourced citizen reporting is an important mechanism for gathering hyper-local situational awareness during coastal emergencies such as storm surges, high wave breaches, tidal inundation, and shoreline erosion. However, emergency operation centres and disaster management agencies frequently encounter operational bottlenecks caused by unverified, noisy, duplicate, or uncalibrated submissions. In this paper, we present the system design, multi-signal feature architecture, and reproducible synthetic benchmark evaluation of \textbf{OceanSaksham}, an open-source platform for coastal hazard reporting featuring an explainable, human-in-the-loop heuristic triage layer and cryptographically hash-chained audit logging, alongside an offline machine learning benchmark harness for future data-driven scoring.

The proposed triage engine formulates 19 features across seven modalities: geospatial GPS precision, camera EXIF metadata cross-validation, temporal submission latency, multilingual Indic keyword alignment (across nine coastal Indian languages), exact-match cryptographic media hashing (SHA-256), spatial-temporal neighborhood corroboration, and model-derived sea-state cross-validation via Open-Meteo marine data. To evaluate triage mechanics under controlled conditions, we formulate a 5-seed parameterized synthetic benchmark ($N=1,000$ per seed) evaluated under a region-held-out spatial split (training on West Coast regions, evaluating on East Coast regions). Across five independent dataset generations, regularized logistic regression achieves $F_1 = 0.946 \pm 0.004$ ($\text{AUC-ROC} = 0.988 \pm 0.001$), random forest achieves $F_1 = 0.938 \pm 0.009$ ($\text{AUC-ROC} = 0.987 \pm 0.002$), and gradient-boosted decision trees achieve $F_1 = 0.934 \pm 0.012$ ($\text{AUC-ROC} = 0.986 \pm 0.003$). While the deployed console runs the explainable JavaScript heuristic rule score, the benchmark indicates that rule heuristics match offline ML models on synthetic data. In a 60-second sustained load benchmark over SQLite, the backend sustained 42 mixed read/write requests/second ($p_{50}=365.75\text{ ms}$). We detail the complete system pipeline, provide climatological marine sanity checks against historical storm events, and discuss privacy design considerations informed by India's DPDP Act 2023.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Coastal communities across India's 7,516~km shoreline face continuous threats from severe oceanographic and meteorological hazards, including cyclonic storm surges, extreme swell waves, tidal floods, and rapid shoreline erosion~\cite{kumar2012incois, mohapatra2012cyclone}. While institutional remote sensing networks and coastal radar stations operated by agencies such as the Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (INCOIS) provide regional forecasts, hyper-local impact assessments (such as breached sea walls, submerged village roads, or fishing harbour distress) rely on participatory crowdsourcing~\cite{okolloh2009ushahidi, sakaki2010earthquake}.

However, raw citizen submissions present severe trust and filtering challenges for emergency dispatchers~\cite{castillo2011information, imran2015processing}. During acute marine emergencies, dispatch queues become congested with unverified reports, recycled duplicate images from past disasters~\cite{gupta2013faking}, inaccurate GPS readings, and well-intentioned false alarms. Furthermore, in multilingual regions spanning official languages such as Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Gujarati, Malayalam, Odia, and English, uniform manual text triage is slow and error-prone.

To address these challenges, this paper presents the engineering architecture, multi-signal feature formulation, and reproducible synthetic evaluation of \textbf{OceanSaksham}, focusing on four core contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Open-Source Full-Stack Architecture}: An end-to-end platform featuring token-bucket rate limiting, Server-Sent Events (SSE) broadcasting, spatial duplicate detection ($<2\text{km}, <3\text{h}$), cryptographically hash-chained append-only audit logging of official decisions, and a multi-state SOS alert workflow.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Signal Feature Formulation}: A 19-dimensional feature representation spanning seven modalities: geospatial GPS precision, photo EXIF cross-validation, temporal urgency, multilingual Indic hazard keyword alignment, exact-match media hashing, spatial-temporal neighborhood corroboration, and model-derived sea-state cross-validation via Open-Meteo marine data.
    \item \textbf{Explainable Human-in-the-Loop Console}: A dispatch dashboard that ranks incoming reports by credibility while exposing top positive and negative signal contributions, preserving complete operator authority while logging official overrides for model feedback.
    \item \textbf{Parameterized Synthetic Benchmark \& Baseline Harness}: A multi-seed synthetic evaluation harness and region-held-out spatial testbed across 14 coastal regions for systematic baseline benchmarking and future evaluation with real pilot data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work \& Platform Comparison}
\subsection{Crisis Informatics \& Participatory Sensing}
Crowdsourcing platforms such as Ushahidi demonstrated the utility of citizen crisis mapping during the 2010 Haiti earthquake~\cite{okolloh2009ushahidi}. In geophysics, the USGS \textit{``Did You Feel It?''} (DYFI) system established citizen reports as a valuable complement to physical seismograph arrays~\cite{wald1999utilization}. Sakaki et al.~\cite{sakaki2010earthquake} utilized social media streams as real-time event sensors for earthquake detection. However, raw social streams often lack structured telemetry, location verification, and spam resistance~\cite{reuter2018fifteen}.

\subsection{Credibility Assessment \& Disaster NLP}
Information credibility classification has traditionally leveraged propagation graphs and linguistic sentiment~\cite{castillo2011information}. Gupta et al.~\cite{gupta2013faking} demonstrated that over 86\% of disaster images during Hurricane Sandy were recycled fakes, emphasizing the necessity of media hashing and EXIF validation. Recent benchmarks such as HumAID~\cite{alam2021humaid} and CrisisNLP~\cite{imran2015processing} have expanded text filtering, while IndicNLP~\cite{kakkar2020indicbert} advanced Indic text processing. Our work builds upon these principles by embedding real-time model-derived marine sea-state data ($H_s$, wind speed) directly into the triage decision space.

\section{System Architecture \& Incident Pipeline}
\subsection{System Architecture}
As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}, the platform comprises three tiers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Citizen Web Client}: Mobile-responsive interface built with React 18 and Vite, supporting Web App Manifest installation, 9 Indic languages, camera EXIF extraction, and GPS capture.
    \item \textbf{Backend REST \& SSE Service}: Node.js/Express service with SQLite storage in development (with spatial Haversine queries and compound indices, designed for migration to PostgreSQL/PostGIS in production), token-bucket rate limiting (max 30 req/min), spatial duplicate checking, and Server-Sent Events broadcasting.
    \item \textbf{Official Dispatch Console}: Command dashboard displaying ranked reports, explainable signal attribution badges, SOS alert monitoring, and danger zone hotspot management.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{End-to-End Incident Pipeline Walkthrough}
The system operates through an end-to-end automated pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Citizen Ingestion}: A citizen captures a photo and submits a hazard report with GPS coordinates and localized description.
    \item \textbf{Duplicate Screening}: The backend computes the image SHA-256 hash and checks against existing reports within $\Delta r \le 2.0\text{ km}$ and $\Delta t \le 3.0\text{ h}$. If matched, the report is linked to the primary incident to prevent queue congestion.
    \item \textbf{Environmental Grounding}: The backend queries the Open-Meteo Marine Weather API~\cite{openmeteo2024marine} for offshore wave height ($H_s$) and wind speed at the nearest maritime grid point.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Signal Scoring}: The 19-dimensional feature vector is evaluated. The live Node.js web backend runs an explainable 19-signal heuristic rule score in JavaScript for in-memory dispatch triage, while the Python pipeline provides the offline machine learning benchmark and feature ablation harness. The benchmark's rule baseline and the deployed JavaScript triage engine implement the same multi-signal weighted heuristic (evaluating GPS error, EXIF distance delta, sea-state compatibility, and neighbor corroboration).
    \item \textbf{Operator Decision \& Cryptographic Audit Trail}: Official operators review the ranked queue on the dispatch console. Any status transition (\texttt{VERIFIED}, \texttt{REJECTED}) generates a hash-chained record in \texttt{audit\_logs} where $h_i = \text{SHA-256}(\text{id} \parallel \text{report\_id} \parallel \text{official\_id} \parallel \text{action} \parallel \text{timestamp} \parallel h_{i-1})$, enabling detection of retroactive log modifications.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Credibility Triage Feature Formulation}
We formulate a 19-dimensional feature vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{19}$ across seven groups:

\textbf{1. Location Group ($\mathbf{f}_{\text{loc}} \in \mathbb{R}^4$)}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x_1 = \ln(1 + \text{accuracy}_{\text{GPS}})$: Log-normalised GPS error radius.
    \item $x_2 = d_{\text{Haversine}}((\text{lat}_{\text{GPS}}, \text{lng}_{\text{GPS}}), (\text{lat}_{\text{EXIF}}, \text{lng}_{\text{EXIF}}))$: Spatial discrepancy between reported location and camera metadata.
    \item $x_3 \in \{0, 1\}$: Binary indicator of valid EXIF GPS presence.
    \item $x_4$: Minimum distance to canonical coastline anchor points.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{2. Time Group ($\mathbf{f}_{\text{time}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$)}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x_5 = \ln(1 + |t_{\text{server}} - t_{\text{device}}| / 60)$: Submission latency in minutes.
    \item $x_6 = \sin(2\pi h / 24), x_7 = \cos(2\pi h / 24)$: Diurnal solar cycle encoding.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3. Text Group ($\mathbf{f}_{\text{text}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$)}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x_8 = \ln(1 + \text{len}(\text{desc}))$: Description character volume.
    \item $x_9 \in \{0, 1\}$: Keyword match against localized hazard terminology across Indic lexicons.
    \item $x_{10} \in \{0, 1\}$: Language indicator (English vs. vernacular).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{4. Media Group ($\mathbf{f}_{\text{media}} \in \mathbb{R}^2$)}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x_{11} \in \{0, 1\}$: Attached media presence.
    \item $x_{12} \in \{0, 1\}$: Duplicate exact-match SHA-256 media hash match.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{5. Corroboration Group ($\mathbf{f}_{\text{corrob}} \in \mathbb{R}^2$)}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x_{13} = \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbb{I}[d(r_i, r_j) \le 5\text{km} \land |t_i - t_j| \le 6\text{h}]$: Independent nearby report count.
    \item $x_{14}$: Hazard-type agreement ratio among neighbors.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{6. Reporter Reputation ($\mathbf{f}_{\text{rep}} \in \mathbb{R}^2$)}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x_{15}$: Historical verification rate.
    \item $x_{16} = \ln(1 + N_{\text{total}})$: Historical report volume.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{7. External Marine Weather ($\mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$)}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x_{17}$: Significant wave height ($H_s$ in meters) retrieved from Open-Meteo Marine API.
    \item $x_{18}$: Coastal wind speed (km/h).
    \item $x_{19} = H_s \times \text{Severity}_{\text{reported}}$: Sea-state consistency interaction term.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Methodology \& Benchmark Suite}
\subsection{Synthetic Dataset Generation \& Threat Models}
Due to the absence of public, verified crowdsourced coastal disaster datasets containing multi-modal telemetry and EXIF metadata, we developed a parameterized synthetic benchmark suite across 14 coastal regions (7 West Coast: Mumbai, South Gujarat, North Gujarat, Goa, Coastal Karnataka, North Kerala, South Kerala; 7 East Coast: Chennai, South Tamil Nadu, Andhra Central, Visakhapatnam, Odisha South, Odisha North, West Bengal).

Each synthetic seed ($N=1,000$) simulates realistic threat conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adverse Weather GPS Degradation}: Genuine storm surge submissions with degraded GPS accuracy ($\pm 50\text{--}150\text{m}$).
    \item \textbf{Messaging App Metadata Stripping}: 50\% of authentic images simulated without EXIF tags, reflecting typical messaging compression.
    \item \textbf{Adversarial Hoaxes}: High-precision inland reports contradicted by calm sea telemetry ($H_s < 1.3\text{m}$) and coastal distance.
    \item \textbf{Region-Held-Out Spatial Partition}: 578 West Coast reports for training; 422 East Coast reports reserved exclusively for testing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Backend Throughput \& Sustained Load Benchmark}
To evaluate backend performance under concurrent load, we executed a 60-second sustained benchmark against the Node.js/SQLite service on an Intel Core i5-12450HX host (8 physical cores: 4P+4E, 12 threads, 16GB RAM) over local loopback (client and server sharing host resources). The test simulated 20 concurrent worker clients submitting a mixed workload composed of 70\% credibility queue queries (\texttt{GET /api/reports?sort\_by=credibility}) and 30\% authenticated report submissions (\texttt{POST /api/reports}) requiring payload validation, spatial duplicate querying, and feature calculation. To measure raw transaction limits, rate limiting was bypassed via pre-authorized test headers, starting from a base table of 50 seeded reports and growing to $\approx 818$ reports.

Across 2,563 completed requests (zero errors, 0.00\% failure rate), the backend sustained an overall throughput of \textbf{42 requests/second}, with a mean latency of $470.28\text{ ms}$, median $p_{50} = 365.75\text{ ms}$, $p_{95} = 949.08\text{ ms}$, and $p_{99} = 1,163.91\text{ ms}$. Throughput across 10-second windows exhibited steady performance under serialized SQLite table write locks: $44\text{ req/s}$ (0--10s), $47\text{ req/s}$ (10--20s), $43\text{ req/s}$ (20--30s), $42\text{ req/s}$ (30--40s), $41\text{ req/s}$ (40--50s), and $38\text{ req/s}$ (50--60s). Adding compound spatial and temporal indices (\texttt{idx\_reports\_coords\_time}) stabilized query scanning as the table expanded.

\subsection{Climatological Marine Sanity Check \& Extreme Events}
To verify the physical realism of the Open-Meteo marine wave height thresholds ($H_s < 1.3\text{m}$ for calm sea-states vs. $H_s > 2.5\text{m}$ for rough/storm sea-states), we queried the Open-Meteo Marine Archive API~\cite{openmeteo2024marine} across coastal coordinates for two severe Indian tropical cyclones alongside 5-year climatological same-date baselines (2018--2022):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cyclone Biparjoy} (Landfall near Jakhau, Gujarat on 15 June 2023; $23.23^\circ\text{N}, 68.60^\circ\text{E}$): Numerical wave model reanalysis yielded mean $H_s = 4.40\text{m}$ ($\max H_s = 4.66\text{m}$), triggering the rough/severe marine threshold ($>2.5\text{m}$). The 5-year climatological same-date baseline (15 June 2018--2022) at the same coordinate yielded mean $H_s = 1.36\text{m}$ ($\max H_s = 1.40\text{m}$).
    \item \textbf{Cyclone Michaung} (Tracking close along the Chennai coast, Tamil Nadu on 3--4 December 2023 before making landfall near Bapatla, Andhra Pradesh on 5 December 2023; $13.08^\circ\text{N}, 80.27^\circ\text{E}$): Model output on 4 December 2023 yielded mean $H_s = 2.54\text{m}$ (marginal around the threshold, $\max H_s = 3.34\text{m}$), whereas the 5-year climatological same-date baseline (4 December 2018--2022) yielded mean $H_s = 1.11\text{m}$.
\end{itemize}
Open-Meteo provides gridded numerical wave model estimates (based on ECMWF WAM and NOAA GFS reanalyses under ODbL terms) rather than in-situ physical buoy measurements. This check indicates that our heuristic thresholds behave in a manner consistent with model-derived wave distributions during verified storm events versus multi-year coastal climatology.

\section{Limitations \& Ethical Considerations}
\textbf{Limitations}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Benchmark Constraints}: All quantitative classification metrics in this paper are derived from a synthetic benchmark testbed. While the generator incorporates domain threat models, performance on real-world disaster streams must be validated in future field deployments.
    \item \textbf{Absence of Human Subject Trials}: No usability evaluation with human participants was conducted; operational workflow descriptions reflect author-driven cognitive architectural walkthroughs of the user interface.
    \item \textbf{Prevalence \& Class Skew}: The benchmark exhibits an active hazard prevalence of $61.9\% \pm 3.7\%$, which is substantially higher than typical non-crisis operational streams where genuine emergencies may represent a small minority of incoming reports.
    \item \textbf{Marine Model Spatial Resolution}: Open-Meteo marine models interpolate offshore open-water grid cells; localized coastal structures (harbour breakwaters, shallow reef estuaries) may exhibit hydrodynamic variations not captured by open-ocean wave models.
    \item \textbf{SOS Emergency Dispatch}: The SOS emergency module is a prototype interface workflow and does not interface directly with national emergency dispatch services (such as India's 112 system).
    \item \textbf{Database-Level Audit Resistance}: The SHA-256 hash chain reliably detects retroactive tampering within the database table; however, adversaries with full root database write access could recompute the entire forward chain. External anchoring of tip hashes or keyed HMACs would provide root-level tampering resistance.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Privacy Design Considerations}:
The platform incorporates privacy protections informed by India's Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act 2023~\cite{dpdpact2023india}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Minimization \& Spatial Coarsening}: Public citizen map views coarsen location coordinates to 2 decimal places ($\approx 1.1\text{ km}$ precision) to prevent exposing private residences.
    \item \textbf{Purpose Limitation \& Pseudonymization}: User identifiers are tokenized using salted SHA-256 hashes in public-facing SSE event broadcasts.
    \item \textbf{Hash-Chained Audit Trail}: Official triage actions are stored in an append-only cryptographic hash chain to ensure verifiable operator oversight.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion \& Future Work}
We presented OceanSaksham, an open-source architecture for credibility-aware triage and audit logging in crowdsourced coastal hazard management. By coupling multi-signal feature engineering with real-time oceanographic validation and cryptographic audit trails, the platform offers an explainable, human-in-the-loop dispatch environment. Future work includes field piloting with regional disaster management authorities and INCOIS, collecting real citizen report streams, and exploring on-device edge ML for low-connectivity coastal fishing operations.
